What research says

What students lose when they miss school

Long suspensions make it harder to graduate.

In New York City, high schoolers who were sent home were less likely to pass both math and English the next year. Being sent home for more than 20 days lowered the chance of graduating on time. Classmates didn't do any better either.

American Institutes for Research, 2021

Stricter schools sent fewer students to college.

Students placed in stricter middle schools were 11% less likely to go to a four-year college, and nobody's test scores went up. This study shows strict discipline causes harm, not just that the two go together.

Bacher-Hicks, Billings and Deming, 2024

What research says

How suspensions are linked to later arrests

Texas: nearly three times the chance of juvenile justice contact.

A suspension nearly tripled (2.85 times) a student's chance of contact with the juvenile justice system the next year.

Council of State Governments Justice Center, Breaking Schools' Rules, 2011

North Carolina: stricter schools, more arrests as adults.

Students assigned to much stricter schools were 15 to 20% more likely to be arrested and incarcerated as adults. This is the strongest evidence of cause.

Bacher-Hicks, Billings and Deming, 2024

Part 4

What SNS would change

In , Westchester children in pre-K to 3rd grade were suspended, and students were suspended 20 days or more. Solutions Not Suspensions would end the first and cap the second.

The bill

What the Solutions Not Suspensions Act Would Change,
Westchester County,

Cap suspensions at 20 school days

students were suspended 20 days or more.

No suspensions in pre-K to grade 3*

children in pre-K to grade 3 were suspended; of them were sent home, for school days.

Schoolwork during a suspension

school days were spent sent home: as many as students missing a whole school year.

No suspensions for minor misbehavior: being late, out of dress code or talking back ("insubordination") could no longer get a child sent home. Westchester's number can't be counted: NYSED's data doesn't say why a student was suspended.

The bill also adds restorative options to codes of conduct and holds charter schools to the same rules.

Alongside the bill, the coalition asks for $50 million in the state budget for school climate: how safe and supported students feel at school.

    About the data

    Sources. Suspension figures are from the New York State Education Department (NYSED), released through a Freedom of Information Law request (FL-EM-25-1398) filed by a partner organization and shared with WCA. They cover every Westchester school district from to . Felony arrest figures are from the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services (DCJS), shared with a youth justice coalition WCA belongs to. District outlines are from Westchester County GIS.

    How to read the numbers. "Sent home" means an out-of-school suspension. A figure "out of 100" is the number of students sent home at least once during the school year, for every 100 students enrolled on NYSED's October count day. Westchester's totals include the county's special act districts and charter schools.

    Small numbers. A group's figure isn't shown when fewer than students are in it, or when NYSED hid the count to protect students' privacy. The page then says "too few students to show".

    Years. School years run from September to June. Schools closed in March 2020 and taught mostly remotely in 2020-21, so those two years had far fewer suspensions.

    Part 3's chart. Each bar is a group's share of everyone at that step: of all students, of students suspended (in school or at home), of students sent home, of students suspended 20 days or more, and of felony arrests of people under 18 ( added together). Arrests are counted, not people, and a share of arrests is not an arrest rate. The steps count different young people, not one group followed over time. "All other students" are Asian, Multiracial, American Indian or Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander students; the arrest records have no Multiracial category, so that group's arrests are those recorded as Asian, other or unknown race.

    What the data can't show. It can't follow one child from a suspension to an arrest, so it can't show that one causes the other; it doesn't say why students were suspended; and it has no count of young people detained or incarcerated by race for Westchester, so part 3 shows arrests.
