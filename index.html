What research says

What students lose when they miss school

Long suspensions hurt graduation.

In New York City, an out-of-school suspension of 21 days or more lowered on-time graduation by 5 percentage points. Harsher discipline did not improve classmates' behavior, attendance or achievement either.

American Institutes for Research (LiCalsi and others), 2021

Missed class time adds up.

Missing 10 classes lowered students' test scores by a small but real amount. Ten absences in 9th grade cut on-time graduation and college enrollment by about 2%. In Westchester, lost the most days of any grade ().

Liu, Lee and Gershenson, 2021

Suspensions are linked to lower test scores and graduation. Westchester's numbers can't show that one causes the other; the research makes that case.

What research says

How suspensions are linked to later arrests

Texas: nearly three times the chance of juvenile justice contact.

A suspension nearly tripled (2.85 times) a student's chance of contact with the juvenile justice system the next year.

Council of State Governments Justice Center, Breaking Schools' Rules, 2011

North Carolina: stricter schools, more arrests as adults.

Students assigned to much stricter schools were 15 to 20% more likely to be arrested and incarcerated as adults. This is the strongest evidence of cause.

Bacher-Hicks, Billings and Deming, 2024

Part 4

What SNS would change

In , Westchester children in pre-K to 3rd grade were suspended, and students were suspended 20 days or more. Solutions Not Suspensions would end the first and cap the second.

The bill

The Solutions Not Suspensions Act, beside Westchester's numbers

The Judith S. Kaye Solutions Not Suspensions Act (S.134 Jackson / A.118 Solages) · Westchester numbers are for

Each card pairs one thing the bill asks with the Westchester number it would change.

Cap suspensions at 20 school days (now up to 180)

No student sent away for most of a school year.

Bar suspensions for minor misbehavior: lateness, dress code, "insubordination"

Being late, out of dress code or talking back can't get a child sent home.

Can't be counted for Westchester: NYSED's data doesn't say why a student was suspended.

Require schoolwork during a suspension

A suspended student keeps learning.

school days were spent sent home, plus days of in-school suspension.

Add restorative options to codes of conduct

Repair harm (talking it through, making amends) instead of only punishing.

Research: Pittsburgh's restorative practices cut suspension days 36%, against 18% in comparison schools, though test scores did not rise (RAND, 2018).

    About the data

    Sources. Suspension figures are from the New York State Education Department (NYSED), released through a Freedom of Information Law request (FL-EM-25-1398) filed by a partner organization and shared with WCA. They cover every Westchester school district from to . Felony arrest figures are from the NYS Division of Criminal Justice Services (DCJS), shared with a youth justice coalition WCA belongs to. District outlines are from Westchester County GIS.

    How to read the numbers. "Sent home" means an out-of-school suspension. A figure "out of 100" is the number of students sent home at least once during the school year, for every 100 students enrolled on NYSED's October count day. Westchester's totals include the county's special act districts and charter schools.

    Small numbers. A group's figure isn't shown when fewer than students are in it, or when NYSED hid the count to protect students' privacy. The page then says "too few students to show".

    Years. School years run from September to June. Schools closed in March 2020 and taught mostly remotely in 2020-21, so those two years had far fewer suspensions.

    What the data can't show. It can't follow one child from a suspension to an arrest; it doesn't say why students were suspended; and it has no count of young people detained or incarcerated by race for Westchester, so part 3 shows arrests.
